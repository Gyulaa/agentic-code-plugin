# Copy: clear first, then persuasive, always honest

Clarity is the base of persuasion. People scan rather than read, and in Nielsen and Morkes's classic study concise, scannable and objective text together made a site 124% more usable. Text that is easy to process is also judged more true (processing fluency). Within five seconds a visitor must know what this is, who it is for, and what to do next. No technique rescues a confusing offer.

## The order of work
1. **Where does the visitor come from and what do they already know?** (Eugene Schwartz's awareness levels.) Unaware or problem-aware traffic (an ad, a broad search) needs the problem first; someone who searched for the brand needs the offer and the price.
2. **The customer's own words.** Reviews, support e-mails, sales-call notes, forum threads. Ask the user for them; if there are none, say the copy rests on assumptions.
3. **Voice and form of address** (below), recorded in `DESIGN.md`.
4. **Structure by mode** (Persuade, Operate, Read, Experience; see `SKILL.md`).
5. **Levers that hold up** (below), only true ones.
6. **The self-check:** Never and the copy tells.

## Voice and tone
- Place the voice on the four axes of Nielsen Norman Group: funny to serious, casual to formal, irreverent to respectful, enthusiastic to matter-of-fact. Write the position into `DESIGN.md`.
- **Voice is constant, tone follows the moment:** the same brand can be light in an empty state and plain in a payment error or a privacy text.
- **The occasion decides:** health, money, legal matters and grief are serious and respectful; leisure products can be casual; business buyers want matter-of-fact language in their trade's vocabulary. Humour is the riskiest axis: only when the brief or existing material shows it.
- **Form of address** is the first tone decision in languages that have one (German du/Sie, French tu/vous, Hungarian tegezés/magázás). Ask, don't guess, and keep it consistent on buttons, errors and e-mails.

## Structure of a persuasive page
A starting point, rearranged by awareness level:
1. What it is and who it is for, in the visitor's words.
2. The problem as they would describe it.
3. What they get and what changes for them: benefits, made concrete ("the VAT return is ready in ten minutes", not "automated tax workflows").
4. Proof: real work, real numbers, real names, with permission.
5. Objections answered: price, risk, time, "will it work for me?".
6. One clear next step, and what happens after it.

## Levers that hold up
Use only what is true. The evidence column says how much to expect; none of these rescues a weak offer.
| Lever | Evidence | On the page |
|---|---|---|
| A recommended default | strong (meta-analysis, d about 0.68, large variance); works when read as advice | the recommended plan is pre-selected with the reason ("enough for most households"); never against the user (a pre-ticked consent is unlawful) |
| Order and anchoring | strong in replications | the order of prices and the first number seen matter; reference prices must be real |
| A reason ("because") | Langer 1978: for larger requests only a real reason works | next to the button, the real consequence: time, cost, what happens next |
| Two-sided message | meta-analysis: admitting a limit raises credibility, if the answer follows | "who this is not for", honest limits, then the answer |
| Lower risk and uncertainty | core of decision research | guarantee, trial, free assessment, number of steps, what happens after the click |
| Show, don't tell | demonstration | photos of real work, before and after, product screenshots, a short demo |
| One consistent claim | fluency, repetition | the same main claim, in the same words, in headline, subhead and button |
| A small first step | commitment and consistency | a calculator or a two-question form before the big request |
| Let them build it | IKEA effect | a configurator or price calculator |
| Specifics | concrete claims are more credible | numbers, places, names and dates, only if true; overly precise numbers from small samples look fake |
| Real faces | eye-tracking: generic stock photos are ignored | the real team and real customers |
| Social proof | works, but conditional: describing an unwanted behaviour as common increases it | verifiable proof (name, place, result), never "thousands of happy customers" |
Choice overload is contested; two to four plans with one recommended is simply good practice. Generic "nudges" show close to zero effect after correcting for publication bias: test, don't promise.

## Interface copy
- Buttons name the action and its result ("Get a free assessment", "Save changes"), not "Submit" or "OK"; the same thing keeps the same name through the whole flow.
- Errors say what happened and how to fix it, without blame; empty states say what will be here and how to start.
- Short sentences, active voice, the user's words, no internal jargon.

## Language and localisation
- Translation is not localisation: the text must think in the target language (word order, longer words in buttons, local examples, units, date and number formats).
- Typographic conventions per language: quotes („…" in Hungarian, «…» in French), decimal commas, non-breaking spaces before units.
- Leave room: German and Hungarian labels run 30% or more longer than English.

## Never
These are unlawful in the EU (Unfair Commercial Practices Directive; the Omnibus Directive for reviews and price reductions; the planned Digital Fairness Act names them again), and visitors recognise them, which destroys the trust persuasion rests on.
- Fake urgency or scarcity: a countdown that restarts, "only 2 left" when untrue.
- Invented reviews, customers, numbers or logos; reviews presented as verified when they are not; a "was" price that is not the lowest of the previous 30 days.
- Confirmshaming ("No, I don't want to save money"), hidden costs, cancelling harder than signing up, pre-ticked consent.
- Hiding the price or process on purpose to force a call (a business choice the user may make, never a default suggestion).
- Placeholders that look real: write `[customer name]`, not a plausible invented person.

## Copy tells (self-check)
One tell is not proof; a cluster is.
- **Inflated vocabulary:** revolutionary, seamless, unlock, elevate, supercharge, game-changer, cutting-edge, next-level, effortless, world-class, "a testament to", "in the world of". The same in other languages (in Hungarian: forradalmi, zökkenőmentes, új szintre emel, páratlan élmény).
- **Structural tics:** "not just X, but Y"; everything in threes; an em dash where a comma belongs; a summary sentence closing every section; question headings the text never answers.
- **Empty generality:** a sentence true of any company ("Customer satisfaction is our priority"). Test: if it stays true with a competitor's name in it, cut it or make it specific.
- **Even mood:** every sentence the same length and the same enthusiasm. Human text varies, and has matter-of-fact parts.

## The visitor chain
Winning a customer is a chain of steps, and it breaks at its weakest link: arrive, understand (five seconds), believe (proof), act (the button), complete (the form), know what happens next (the confirmation). For each step name the element responsible and its weak point. The best hero does not save a confusing form. `design-review` walks this chain.
